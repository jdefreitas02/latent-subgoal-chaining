\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
A defining trend in modern machine learning is the rise of general-purpose models trained using self-supervised objectives on large-scale, unlabelled datasets \cite{ogbench_park2025}. This paradigm has proven highly effective, largely because unannotated data is abundant and inexpensive to collect, enabling models to scale without the bottleneck of manual labelling. In contrast, reinforcement learning (RL) has traditionally relied on a very different paradigm: agents learn through interaction with an environment, guided by carefully designed, task-specific reward functions. This reliance on online interaction makes RL both computationally expensive and difficult to deploy safely in real-world settings such as robotics, where exploration can be costly or even dangerous.

Offline reinforcement learning offers a promising alternative by learning entirely from previously collected datasets, avoiding the need for further interaction. Within this setting, goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (GCRL) provides a natural framework for learning general-purpose behaviours. The idea of learning to achieve multiple goals has long been studied in RL \cite{Kaelbling1993LearningTA} and was later formalised through architectures such as Universal Value Function Approximators \cite{pmlr-v37-schaul15}; offline GCRL builds on these ideas by training agents to reach arbitrary states using static datasets of past experience \cite{ogbench_park2025}. Despite this conceptual simplicity, learning to reach goals from offline data is challenging in practice: in long-horizon, continuous-control tasks, reward signals are often sparse, and offline learning suffers from the distribution shift between the behaviour policy that generated the data and the policy that is being learned.

Model-based reinforcement learning offers a potential solution. By learning a model of the environment dynamics, an agent can simulate future trajectories and plan using imagined rollouts \cite{ha2018worldmodels, hafner2020dreamer, hafner2025dreamerv4}. The most direct version of this idea uses a learned latent world model in place of additional environment interaction. How effective that substitution is in practice --- across goal-conditioned and single-task offline RL, across different training-time and inference-time uses of the model --- is the question this thesis tries to answer.

\section{Thesis question}

We test different ways to apply a single frozen latent world model (LeWM, \S\ref{sec:lewm-bg}) to offline reinforcement learning, in both goal-conditioned and single-task settings. The world model is asked to play three roles, and the methodology chapters are organised by which role they pursue:
\begin{itemize}
    \item As a \emph{training-time environment substitute} (Chapter~\ref{chap:wm-as-env}), in which the predictor stands in for the real simulator during goal-conditioned online RL.
    \item As a \emph{grounder for subgoal proposals} (Chapter~\ref{chap:wm-as-subgoal}), in which short WM rollouts are used to evaluate candidate subgoals inside a hierarchical offline RL agent.
    \item As an \emph{inference-time planner} for the offline-to-online phase of single-task RL (Chapter~\ref{chap:online-with-wm}), in which the WM scores candidate action chunks under a learned $Q$-function.
\end{itemize}
The first role largely fails. The second works in HIQL+LeWM as long as the WM is used only at evaluation, and fails when the WM is used inside the training loop (WGSP). The third works.

\section{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{World-model–based training techniques operating entirely in the JEPA latent space.} We learn $Q$-functions, hierarchical value functions, flow-matching policies, and MPC-distilled actors all in the same $192$-dimensional latent space induced by a single frozen LeWM, and compare them under a unified offline-RL setup (Chapters~\ref{chap:wm-as-env}--\ref{chap:online-with-wm}).
    \item \emph{A catalogue of failure modes when the world model is used as a training environment.} Across the goal-conditioned SAC family (Chapter~\ref{chap:wm-as-env}) and the offline-to-online setting (Chapter~\ref{chap:online-with-wm} §\ref{sec:owm-exp1}--§\ref{sec:owm-synthesis}), we identify four distinct failure mechanisms --- compounding latent error, $Q$-function corruption, termination-threshold pathology, and the model-exploitation trap --- and provide the diagnostic chain that isolates each one.
    \item \emph{Offline-learned $Q$-functions are a useful in-distribution indicator for world-model latents.} The empirical observation underpinning Chapter~\ref{chap:online-with-wm}: where the offline-trained $Q$-function assigns high values and low ensemble disagreement, the WM's predictions are reliable enough for short-horizon planning; where it assigns low values or high disagreement, the WM is in OOD territory. This is what makes FMQ inference (a gradient step under the offline $Q$-function) a usable OOD-aware search procedure even though it never explicitly models the WM's uncertainty.
    \item \emph{FMQ-MPC for offline-to-online RL.} The headline positive result: using FMQ-guided MPC as the offline-to-online fine-tuning technique --- combining short-horizon WM rollouts, $Q$-gradient trust-region action refinement, and policy updates that incorporate those refined actions --- reaches $96.0\%$ on \texttt{visual-cube-single-singletask-task1-v0}, a $+3.2\%$ absolute lift over the OGBench IMPALA reference, without collecting a single real-env transition in the post-offline phase. The interleaved MPC-label distillation that produces a critic compatible with fine FMQ inference is a component of this pipeline.
\end{itemize}
