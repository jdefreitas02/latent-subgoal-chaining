\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation}
A defining trend in modern machine learning is the rise of
general-purpose models trained using self-supervised objectives on
large-scale, unlabelled datasets. This paradigm has proven highly
effective largely because unannotated data is abundant and
inexpensive to collect, enabling models to scale without the
bottleneck of manual labelling
\cite{devlin2019bertpretrainingdeepbidirectional, chen2020simpleframeworkcontrastivelearning, he2021maskedautoencodersscalablevision}.
However, learning from fixed datasets is ultimately limited by the range
and quality of the data that has already been collected; continued
progress may therefore require agents to acquire new experience through
interaction rather than only consuming static human-generated data
\cite{SilverSutton2025}. In contrast, reinforcement learning
has traditionally relied on a different paradigm: agents learn
through interaction with an environment, guided by carefully
designed reward functions \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. This reliance on online interaction
makes RL both computationally expensive and difficult to deploy
safely in real-world settings such as robotics, where exploration
can be costly \cite{kober2013reinforcement,dulacarnold2019challengesrealworldreinforcementlearning}.

Offline reinforcement learning offers an alternative by learning
entirely from previously collected datasets, avoiding the need for
further interaction. Within this setting, goal-conditioned
reinforcement learning provides a natural framework for learning
general-purpose behaviours: train an agent on a static dataset of
past experience and ask it to reach arbitrary states at evaluation
\cite{Kaelbling1993LearningTA, pmlr-v37-schaul15, ogbench_park2025}.
The conceptual simplicity is appealing, but in practice the task is hard:
reward signals in long-horizon continuous-control tasks are sparse,
and offline learning suffers from the distribution shift between
the behaviour policy that generated the data and the policy that
is being learned \cite{levine2020offlinereinforcementlearningtutorial}.

Model-based RL offers a potential solution. By learning a model
of the environment dynamics, an agent can simulate future
trajectories and plan using imagined rollouts
\cite{ha2018worldmodels, hafner2020dreamer, hafner2025dreamerv4}.
The most direct version of this idea uses a learned latent world model
in place of additional environment interaction. Prior work has shown
that learned models can support policy learning through imagined
rollouts, offline model-based optimisation, and value-guided planning
\cite{ha2018worldmodels, hafner2020dreamer, yu2020mopomodelbasedofflinepolicy, hansen2022temporaldifferencelearningmodel}.
However, how reliably a frozen latent world model can provide useful
training or planning signals in offline RL remains an open research
question, particularly when the model is used beyond short-horizon
prediction. This thesis addresses that question by evaluating a single
frozen world model across several uses in single-task and
goal-conditioned offline reinforcement learning.

\section{Thesis Question}

This thesis investigates a central question:

\begin{quote}
\emph{How can a frozen latent world model be used to improve offline reinforcement learning, and under what conditions are its predictions reliable enough to provide useful training or planning signals?}
\end{quote}

To answer this question, we study the use of a latent action-conditioned world model across several roles. In each case, the same underlying offline RL agent is used, allowing differences in performance to be attributed to how the world model is integrated rather than to changes in the reinforcement learning algorithm itself.

The world model is evaluated in four settings:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{As a training environment}
(Chapter~\ref{chap:wm-env}), where the learned dynamics model is used as a direct substitute for the real environment during the online fine-tuning phase. This provides a controlled study of whether imagined experience can replace real interaction.

\item \textbf{As an inference-time planner}
(Chapter~\ref{chap:wm-planner}), where the world model is queried only at decision time. Short imagined rollouts are scored using the offline-trained value function and used to select actions, while policy training remains entirely offline.

\item \textbf{As a source of additional training signal}
(Chapter~\ref{chap:mpc-distillation}), where planner-selected actions and short imagined transitions are converted into policy-improvement data. This tests whether world-model rollouts can be used to generate useful training datasets and supervision targets for the policy.

\item \textbf{As a subgoal evaluator for hierarchical RL}
(Chapter~\ref{chap:latent-subgoal-planning}), where imagined rollouts are used to assess candidate subgoals proposed by a high-level policy. The world model therefore influences behaviour through subgoal selection rather than direct action planning.

\end{itemize}

Together, these experiments map out the boundary between settings where a frozen world model can provide useful decision-making signal and settings where model errors dominate.

\section{Contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{A systematic study of world-model usage in offline reinforcement learning.}
We evaluate the same frozen latent action-conditioned world model across
multiple roles --- environment simulation, inference-time planning, policy
improvement, and hierarchical subgoal selection --- while keeping the underlying
agent architecture and offline RL algorithm fixed. This provides a controlled
comparison of what a modern latent world model can and cannot contribute to
offline RL
(Chapters~\ref{chap:wm-env}--\ref{chap:latent-subgoal-planning}).

\item \textbf{An empirical characterisation of the limitations of world-model-generated training data.}
Directly replacing the environment with the world model consistently degrades performance despite strong one-step prediction accuracy. Through a series of diagnostic experiments, we identify several plausible mechanisms underlying this behaviour, including compounding prediction error, corruption of value-learning targets, and value-aware model exploitation
(Chapter~\ref{chap:wm-env}).

\item \textbf{A positive result for short-horizon value-guided world-model use.} Although long imagined rollouts are unreliable, short-horizon world-model use can still improve policy performance when constrained by an offline-trained critic. We show that value-guided planning provides useful policy-improvement signals without requiring additional real-environment interaction (Chapter~\ref{chap:wm-planner}).

\item \textbf{A model-based policy improvement procedure that operates entirely within the world model.}
Building on the inference-time planner, we introduce an online phase in which planner-selected actions and imagined transitions are used to train the policy while keeping the offline-trained critic fixed. Without test-time planning, the resulting policy reaches
$90.7 \pm 0.8\%$ final success and
$94.8 \pm 0.7\%$ peak success on
\texttt{visual-cube-single-singletask-task1-v0},
corresponding to relative improvements of $11.2\%$ and $7.7\%$ over the corresponding offline baseline success rates ($81.6\%$ and $88.0\%$). The method requires no additional real-environment interaction and does not require the world model at evaluation time
(Chapter~\ref{chap:mpc-distillation}).

\item \textbf{An empirical evaluation of world-model-guided hierarchical subgoal planning.}
We introduce a hierarchical reinforcement learning method that uses imagined rollouts to evaluate candidate subgoals while keeping value estimation grounded in real data. Although the approach does not outperform the offline baseline, the results provide insight into the challenges of combining world-model prediction with latent-space subgoal learning
(Chapter~\ref{chap:latent-subgoal-planning}).

\end{itemize}
