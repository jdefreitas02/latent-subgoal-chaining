
\chapter{Experiments}
\label{chap:experiments}

This chapter evaluates the methods of Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}
on \textsc{OGBench} cube-single-play. We first describe the
experimental setup (\S\ref{sec:exp-setup}), then characterise the
quality of the underlying world model (\S\ref{sec:exp-wm}), and
proceed through the five RL methods in the same order as
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}: curriculum SAC
(\S\ref{sec:exp-curr-sac}), HER+SAC (\S\ref{sec:exp-her-sac}),
hierarchical joint SAC (\S\ref{sec:exp-joint}), HIQL+LeWM
(\S\ref{sec:exp-hiql}), and the flow-matching variant
(\S\ref{sec:exp-flow}). \S\ref{sec:exp-cem} reports an alternative
evaluation in which LeWM is used as the model in a sample-based
planner (CEM/MPC); \S\ref{sec:exp-diag} collects diagnostics; and
\S\ref{sec:exp-discuss} concludes.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\subsection{Environment and Datasets}
We use \textsc{OGBench} cube-single-play, a five-task object-pushing
benchmark with $25$-D continuous actions and either $64{\times}64$ or
$224{\times}224$ RGB observations. Each evaluation runs the five
predefined tasks (\texttt{horizontal}, \texttt{vertical1},
\texttt{vertical2}, \texttt{diagonal1}, \texttt{diagonal2}) for $50$
episodes each, with a maximum of $200$ steps per episode.
World-model and offline-cache training data come from the expert
trajectories distributed with \textsc{OGBench}.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocols}
Three protocols are used.

\paragraph{Native OGBench.} The agent is rolled out in the real
simulator and a task counts as solved when the cube ends within
$4$\,cm of the target (\textsc{OGBench}'s native success criterion).
This is our headline metric and the one reported in all main tables.

\paragraph{Latent goal-reaching (latent-reach rate).} An
in-distribution sanity check in which goals are sampled from training
data and the agent must drive the latent state to within $\delta_d$ of
the goal embedding in $\mathcal{Z}$. During SAC+HER training the agent
never touches MuJoCo: transitions are produced entirely by the LeWM
predictor inside \texttt{LatentEnv}, the environment is teleported to a
cached start latent, and a ``success'' is logged when
$\|z_t - z^\star\|_2 < \delta_d$. The done-threshold is
$\delta_d{=}2.887$ for the $64{\times}64$ sweep and $\delta_d{=}2.0$ for
the legacy $224{\times}224$ stack. The ``\texttt{Success\_Rate}'' column
of every \texttt{training\_metrics.csv} is the running mean of this
Boolean over the last $2{,}000$ attempts. \emph{This latent-reach rate
measures only whether the actor can drive the predictor to a target
latent; it says nothing about whether the cube task is solved, and must
never be compared head-to-head with the native-OGBench rate below.}
Used during training and for the diagnostics of \S\ref{sec:exp-diag}.

\paragraph{CEM/MPC planning.} As an alternative to learning a policy,
we replace the actor with a cross-entropy-method planner that
optimises action sequences against the LeWM rollout. We use $300$
samples, $30$ CEM iterations, top-$30$ selection, planning horizon
$5$, receding horizon $5$, and an action-block of $5$
environment steps per planned action. Reported separately in
\S\ref{sec:exp-cem}.

\subsection{Metrics}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Success rate (\%):} fraction of episodes solved by the
native OGBench criterion. Reported per-task and as a $5$-task average.
\item \textbf{Latent drift:} $\|\hat z_{t+\Delta} - z_{t+\Delta}\|_2$
between the predictor's $\Delta$-step rollout and the encoded ground
truth (\S\ref{sec:exp-wm-drift}).
\item \textbf{Per-step diagnostics:} latent distance to goal and
action norm logged at every step (\S\ref{sec:exp-diag}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sparse vs Dense Reward}
The sparse reward (Eq.~\eqref{eq:her-sparse}) is uninformative but
unbiased: it gives no gradient until a relabelled goal is actually
reached, but when it does fire the signal is exact. The dense potential
reward (Eq.~\eqref{eq:her-dense}) supplies a gradient at every step, but
the clip on the per-step potential introduces a bias toward
short-horizon goal reduction and can reward latent motion that does not
correspond to real cube motion. In the native-OGBench tables below the
dense variant is consistently the stronger of the two
(Tables~\ref{tab:her-sac},~\ref{tab:joint-gap}), but neither approaches
the state-observation upper bound (Table~\ref{tab:headline}).

\subsection{Effect of \texttt{cache\_ratio} and AWR Temperature}
\label{sec:exp-joint-cache}
Mixing offline expert transitions into each SAC batch
(\texttt{cache\_ratio}) together with a sharper AWR temperature
($\beta$) gives the largest single-task gains we observed in the
hierarchical setting. At $G{=}2$ with $\beta{=}20$, raising
\texttt{cache\_ratio} from $0$ to $0.5$ lifts native success from $1.2\%$
to $3.6\%$, driven almost entirely by task~1 (horizontal), which reaches
$18\%$ in isolation; the remaining tasks stay at $0\%$, indicating the
expert mix biases the policy toward the easiest trajectory rather than
broadly improving competence. Configurations with
$\texttt{cache\_ratio}\!\geq\!0.8$ were only run under the latent-reach
protocol (where they scored $0\%$) and are not native-confirmed.

\subsection{State-Observation Hierarchical Control}
\label{sec:exp-joint-state}
To isolate the effect of the JEPA encoder from the hierarchical
algorithm itself, we re-implemented the full two-level architecture
(AWR high-level + SAC low-level) directly on the $28$-D state
observation, with the high-level proposing an intermediate cube position
(\texttt{train\_joint\_state.py}). Despite removing the encoder
bottleneck entirely, both $G{=}5$ and $G{=}10$ variants score $0.0\%$ on
all five native tasks. The failure mode is distinct from the flat state
baseline (which solves three tasks, Table~\ref{tab:headline}): the
high-level proposes poorly-calibrated subgoals early in training, giving
the low-level a noisier effective goal than the direct physical goal,
which triggers premature entropy collapse ($\alpha\!\to\!0.01$ floor
within a few hundred iterations). This indicates the hierarchical
decomposition \emph{itself} is fragile without a reliable high-level
policy, independently of representation quality, and that a warm-started
high-level (\S\ref{sec:exp-joint-hl}) is a prerequisite rather than an
optional refinement.

\section{CEM/MPC with the Learned World Model}
\label{sec:exp-cem}

We replace the learned policy with a CEM planner that optimises
action sequences against LeWM rollouts (settings in
\S\ref{sec:exp-setup}). The two evaluation regimes diverge sharply for
this planner: it reaches $72.0\%$ on the in-distribution
\emph{latent-reach} metric (it is, after all, directly optimising latent
distance to the goal embedding) yet solves $0.0\%$ of the native cube
task. This is the cleanest single demonstration of the central finding
of this chapter --- excellent latent goal-reaching does not imply task
success, because the predictor drift documented in Fig.~\ref{fig:drift}
means a latent trajectory that ends at the goal embedding need not
correspond to a physical trajectory that places the cube.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:exp-discuss}

The single most important finding of this chapter is the gulf between
the two evaluation regimes of \S\ref{sec:exp-setup}. Every method that
optimises latent distance --- SAC+HER ($>\!87\%$ latent-reach,
Table~\ref{tab:sac-train-sr}), the CEM planner ($72\%$ latent-reach,
Table~\ref{tab:cem-baseline}), and the legacy $224$ stack ($>\!95\%$,
Table~\ref{tab:lhrl-train}) --- can drive the predictor to a target
embedding almost perfectly, yet the same policies solve at most a few
percent of the native cube task, and the CEM planner solves none. High
latent-reach is necessary but nowhere near sufficient: a latent
trajectory that terminates at the goal embedding need not correspond to
a physical trajectory that places the cube, precisely because of the
predictor drift in Fig.~\ref{fig:drift}. Any latent-reach number in this
chapter should therefore be read as a training diagnostic, never as a
task result.

The progression from \S\ref{sec:exp-curr-sac} to
\S\ref{sec:exp-flow} maps cleanly onto the algorithmic progression of
Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}. Curriculum SAC fails for lack of a
learning signal on long-horizon tasks; HER+SAC adds the signal but
still scores $0\%$ natively; the hierarchical joint variant reaches a
modest $3.6\%$ at short gaps but collapses once the high-level gap
exceeds the predictor's horizon of fidelity ($G\!\geq\!5$,
Table~\ref{tab:joint-gap}); HIQL+LeWM is the first method to break
double-digit success, and is best when imagination is \emph{disabled}
($\rho_{\mathrm{wu}}=1$, Table~\ref{tab:hiql-lewm-sweep}); the
flow-matching variant
\todo{verify: confirm whether it improves on the Gaussian actor or
not.}

A second, independent fragility is exposed by the state-observation
hierarchical control of \S\ref{sec:exp-joint-state}: even with the
encoder removed entirely, the two-level architecture scores $0\%$
because an unreliable high-level gives the low-level a noisier goal than
a flat policy would, triggering entropy collapse. The hierarchy thus
requires a warm-started high-level (Table~\ref{tab:hl-mlp-train}) as a
prerequisite, not an add-on.

The most informative diagnostic remains the predictor-drift curve in
Fig.~\ref{fig:drift}: methods whose effective horizon exceeds roughly
$10$--$25$ raw frames fall off a cliff, matching the joint-SAC gap
boundary between $G{=}2$ ($10$ frames, $3.6\%$) and $G{=}5$ ($25$
frames, $0\%$). This frames the principal direction for future work as
improving long-horizon predictor fidelity, either by (i) larger or
longer-trained world models, (ii) a contrastive auxiliary objective on
top of the JEPA loss in Eq.~\eqref{eq:pred-loss}, or (iii) abandoning
closed-form roll-outs in favour of a hybrid offline+real-data training
regime in which the world model is consulted only on short horizons.
